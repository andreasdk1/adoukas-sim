40 min until the last of the ice is gone. The drink then takes about 2 h 20 min more to reach 20 °C.
Physics
Heat conduction through glass, ice and drink; melting with latent heat; the ice cubes as floating rigid bodies (buoyancy, weight, drag from the currents, collisions); buoyant flow in the drink driven by both temperature and alcohol content; mixing of meltwater into the cocktail; heat from the room by natural convection, radiation and condensation on the cold glass. The room air around the glass is solved live in your browser.
Role of ML
None. It's a straight physics simulation, computed once in Python and played back here as a time-lapse.

What you're watching

  • The ice: two cubes from a −18 °C freezer, floating about 95 % submerged. Each is a free rigid body: the currents push it around, buoyancy and weight turn it, and it bumps into the glass and the other cube. They warm to 0 °C within the first minute, then melt unevenly, which changes their shape and how they float. At about 29 minutes, with around 15 % of the ice left, the melted-down cubes become unstable and flip, and one is then carried right across the glass by the surface currents.
  • Sinking meltwater: a martini (gin and vermouth, about 30 % alcohol) is lighter than water: about 962 against 1000 kg/m³. The cold, fresh meltwater coming off the ice is the densest liquid in the glass, so it sinks in streams and pools in the bottom of the V. Switch to Dilution to watch the bottom get watered down first: after 20 minutes the top is still at 26 % alcohol while the bottom is down to 24.5 %. By the end the whole drink is at about 24 %, roughly a fifth weaker than poured, and still slightly stronger at the top.
  • Condensation: once the glass drops below the dew point of the room (12.6 °C at 22 °C and 55 % humidity), water condenses on it. That isn't just cosmetic: condensation releases latent heat into the glass and is a large part of what warms the drink.
  • The cold air: air next to the glass cools, gets denser and slides down the bowl and the stem onto the table. The room air is solved live, in real time, using the glass temperature from the simulation.

Setup

A martini glass (3 mm wall) filled to 10 mm below the rim, with the drink poured at 3 °C into a glass at room temperature, plus two 16 mm ice cubes at −18 °C. Room at 22 °C and 55 % relative humidity. Yes, ice in a martini glass is unusual. It just looks better.

Stated limits

  • This is a 2D slice through the glass, so the mechanisms are right and the times are the right order of magnitude, but they're indicative rather than exact for a real 3D glass.
  • The ice melts at 0 °C. In reality ice in alcohol also slowly dissolves slightly below 0 °C. The drink doesn't evaporate, and the small part of the ice above the waterline doesn't exchange heat.
  • Being a 2D slice, the cubes can only turn in the plane you're looking at, which is exactly the tumble you'd see from the side.
  • Flow in the drink is resolved on a 0.75 mm grid: plumes and layering are captured, while the finest eddies are smoothed out.
