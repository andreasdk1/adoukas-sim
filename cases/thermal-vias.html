< 1 % of the temperature rise is all that tenting changes when the free side has a plane, in every case tried. The copper connected to the array decides the junction temperature: pad-only copper against a plane is worth 150 K at 1.5 W, the drill a few kelvin.

Short answer

  • Tenting changes the temperature rise by under 1 % whenever the free side has a plane (0.07–0.8 % across all cases, 0.23 % at default). With pad-only copper on both sides it reaches 2–4 %, in designs that are already far above any junction limit. Open vias perform marginally worse: their pads replace solder mask (emissivity 0.9) with bare finish (0.1), which radiates less.
  • The copper connected to the array sets the temperature. At default (1.5 W, pad-only copper on the part side), a plane on the free side holds the junction at 114 °C; pad-only copper on both sides gives 262 °C, a plane on both 92 °C. Without vias the default reaches 259 °C: with limited copper on the part side, the vias are the only way to the plane.
  • Copper of other nets around the pad carries little heat. Across a 0.1–1 mm clearance gap two nets exchange 0.4–2.6 mW/K per mm of edge, 30–100 times less than continuous copper. A 4 × 4 mm pad ringed by another net at 0.2 mm is 26–91 K/W from it, against 4.4 K/W through the vias; counting only the connected copper is the conservative assumption.
  • The drill is a second-order choice. Across every drill from 0.30 to 1.00 mm the default junction moves by 4.2 K; copper fill gains 5 K.
  • The vias work by conduction. The open holes are dead ends, closed by the solder under the part, and carry 0.2 % of the heat at default, at most about 1 %.
  • Component orientation has little effect in this enclosure: under 1 K between mounting the part on top and underneath with a free-side plane, up to 3 K with pad-only copper.
  • Tenting matters for assembly: open vias in a soldered pad wick solder away from the joint and leave voids.

Rules of thumb

  • Copper first. Connect the array to a plane on at least one side; nothing about the vias themselves comes close to that.
  • Pitch = drill + c, where c is the larger of the hole-to-hole rule and two annular rings (plus any mask web). Each via conducts through its plated barrel, so copper per unit area goes as (d − t)/(d + c)², highest at d ≈ c + 2t (t the plating). Drills below that do not help.
  • Then fit whole rows. Only whole vias fit the pad, so the array resistance is saw-toothed in the drill: take the largest drill that keeps the row count. This is why the calculator lands between the minimum drill and a large one.
  • Filled vias conduct through their whole section: the largest drill that keeps the row count wins.
  • Check the drill against the junction, not the array alone: the calculator solves every drill, and the spread is typically a few kelvin.
  • Tent or plug for assembly; the thermal cost is negligible.

Stated limits

  • Air layers use correlations, about ±20 %; the down-facing side is the least certain.
  • Vias are smeared over the pad; solder voids and wicking are not modelled.
  • Copper of other nets is not counted, which errs on the safe side. Inner planes are lumped into the nearer outer layer; isolated inner planes are left out.
  • Board edges and other parts on the board are left out; with the fan on, the box is vented.
